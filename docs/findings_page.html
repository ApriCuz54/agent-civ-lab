Agent Civilizations · from the lab to the desk

What playing games with agents taught me about using them

I ran a dozen multi-agent experiments — prisoner's dilemmas, pricing duopolies, invasions, reputation — to see how LLM agents behave in numbers. The real question was practical: can any of it make me get more out of an agent day to day? It can, and the gap is large enough to watch happen.

Reasoning is the whole lever. The moment the model may show its working, accuracy jumps to ~99–100% and stays there — a persona, a verify pass, and a 3-agent vote all land in the same place as plainly letting it reason. With reasoning forbidden, none of those add-ons rescue it: flattery, and even three no-reasoning agents voting (at 3× the cost), sit with the bare answer-only bar. Two honest corrections to an earlier version of this chart: a verify pass adds nothing over plain reasoning here, and a fair fan-out (three reasoning agents) is no better than one — the earlier chart had stacked the vote against itself by fanning out the no-reasoning prompt.

The playbook

What to actually do differently

Four habits, each backed by a measured effect rather than a hunch. The demo above proves them directly for tasks with a checkable answer; treat them as well-founded defaults to test on your own work, not universal laws.

Never make it hurry. Let it think on paper.

The single biggest lever. Words like “quick”, “just the answer”, “asap”, “don't overthink” strip out the working the model needs, and accuracy falls off a cliff on anything multi-step.

Do: ask for the reasoning or the working, especially for math, logic, planning, multi-file edits. Don't: optimise your prompt for speed or terseness on a hard task.

this study: 60% → 100%levers: “urgent” dropped code 95% → 90%, swings to −47pts

A verify pass is insurance, not the lever.

“Give your answer, then re-derive it a different way and fix any disagreement” is good hygiene — but be honest about why it helps: it forces the model to reason. On this suite it scored the same as simply letting the model show its working (both ~99%), so it added nothing on top of reasoning itself. It earns its keep on harder tasks where a single reasoning pass still slips, or where the check can be a real executable test.

Do: add a self-check when a wrong answer is costly and cheap to catch. Don't: assume “verify” is doing something a plain reasoning prompt wasn't already.

verify 99% = reason 100%on our own coding suite, in-prompt verify ≈ control (95%)

Delete the pep-talk and the personas.

“You are a world-class expert who never makes mistakes” is the most common thing people paste into prompts. It moved nothing here in either regime: 68% vs 60% with reasoning off (overlapping), and 99% vs 100% with reasoning on. Across the wider study, personas, tips, threats and emotional framing were null — or harmful — above a decent baseline.

Don't: spend your prompt on flattery or role-play. Do: spend those words on the task, the constraints, and the reasoning.

persona: null with reasoning on and offmotivation levers: personas ≈ null

More agents is not more brains. A good single pass matches the fan-out.

Spinning up three agents and majority-voting is the reflex answer to “make it more reliable.” Tested fairly — three reasoning agents voting — it scored exactly the same as one reasoning agent (99%), at three times the cost. And three no-reasoning agents don't rescue a no-reasoning prompt: identical models make correlated mistakes and vote for the same wrong answer. Homogeneous agent “teams” saturate at an effective size of one or two.

Do: fix the single prompt first (let it reason); reach for a genuinely different model or a cited critic before N identical copies.

The wider study · still practical

One sentence can flip an agent's whole disposition

The behavioural games surfaced something you won't see in a one-shot test but will absolutely hit in a long-running system: a single stray phrase in a standing instruction can move an agent from competitive to colluding, or from cooperative to defecting on turn one. Baselines below are already near the extremes, so the lesson is audit what's in your system prompt.

The wider study · for agent builders

Accountability works — until it's gamed

The evolutionary arc has a direct lesson for anyone giving agents memory, reputation, or a shared scratchpad. A lone selfish agent thrives among anonymous cooperators. Make behaviour visible and the group polices it for free. But a reputation that averages over all of history can be farmed.

One always-defect agent out-earns the cooperators it exploits. Cooperation survives but isn't stable — copy the winner and defection spreads.

Show each agent its partner's track record and cooperators punish the known cheat (8% vs 99%) with no instruction to. Accountability restores order.

Trust, then betray
+7…+14
exploit is back

An agent that behaves for a while, banks a clean record, then defects beats the defence at every timing — a lifetime average decays too slowly to catch it.

If your system trusts agents by reputation or history, weight recent behaviour, not a lifetime average. This is now tested, not asserted: swapping the lifetime average for a recency-weighted score (last few actions, or one-strike) collapses the trust-then-betray exploit — the previously-best mid-game betrayal drops from +14 to about zero, and the invader ends up losing. Only an unavoidable last-move defection still pays. And letting strategies actually reproduce over generations confirms the whole arc: defection spreads under anonymity and dies out once reputation is visible.

Honesty

What this is and isn't

Everything here is Claude Haiku 4.5, measured on specific dates, at small scale (2–24 agents, short horizons), mostly with extended thinking off. Cross-model replication — does a stronger model behave the same? — is stated future work, not done here. The demo suite was deliberately chosen at a mid-difficulty band (hard enough that a no-reasoning pass fails, easy enough that reasoning succeeds), so the size of the gap is a property of that choice; the direction is the robust part. This page was rebuilt after a hostile self-review flagged the original for over-claiming — a verify pass and a fan-out were credited with more than the data showed. Treat the playbook as well-founded defaults to test on your own tasks, not universal constants.

The honest one-liner: let the model reason. Almost everything else people reach for — personas, a verify wrapper, more agents — is either downstream of that or does nothing measurable on top of it. Every bar and number here is a few hundred lines of cached, cost-logged Python you can re-run.
